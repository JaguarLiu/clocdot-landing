Why remote attendance

Why distributed teams need something different

Attendance used to be simple: people badged in on arrival, badged out on the way home, and HR pulled a report at month-end. Once remote and hybrid work became normal, that arrangement broke in three specific places:

  • The punch is tied to a device or a network — someone working from home simply cannot use it.
  • Self-reported hours — nobody can tell whether the numbers reflect what actually happened.
  • Office and remote hours land in the same bucket — impossible to separate at month-end.

Most teams patch this with a chat-group routine: post when you start, post when you finish. It works for a while. It stops working somewhere around thirty people, or the first month someone has to calculate overtime from it — the messages are scattered through a conversation, awkward to total up, and they do not amount to a proper attendance record.

The point of a remote attendance system is not surveillance. It is making the hours data automatic, trustworthy and traceable. Employees stop announcing themselves in a channel, managers stop chasing, HR stops reconciling by hand. For the remote employee it cuts both ways too — the time they actually worked is on the record, rather than depending on a manager happening to notice.

And the honest limits of GPS: continuous positioning drains battery; accuracy degrades to tens of metres in basements, inside industrial buildings or among high-rises; and some employees are legitimately uneasy about their employer knowing where they are. So ClocDot takes a single reading at the moment of the punch rather than tracking through the day, and when positioning fails it flags the punch instead of blocking it — the record still gets made, and a human decides what it means. In practice, combining GPS with WiFi verification (network on office days, coordinates on remote days) tends to be the most balanced setup.

Compliance

What an attendance record needs to contain

One of the most common questions we get from overseas HR teams: "our staff work from home and we measure output, so do we still need to track hours?" Under Taiwan's Labor Standards Act, yes — record-keeping obligations do not disappear because the work happens off-site. Working from home, working remotely and working in the field all still need records.

Three things to get right

  • Logged daily, down to the minute. Not "present today" — it needs to be possible to reconstruct the actual start and end of each working day. This is exactly why a chat message saying "starting now" falls short as a record.
  • Retained for a number of years. Records are not something to clear once payroll closes. Paper cards and chat logs carry the most risk here, because you usually find out whether they still exist at the exact moment someone asks to see them.
  • Available to the employee. When an employee asks to review or copy their own attendance record, the employer is expected to provide it. The system therefore has to show employees their own history — not just an HR back office.

As a buying checklist, that is three questions: is the record granular enough, does it survive long enough, and can the employee retrieve it?

The three hard parts, solved

"How do we know they actually worked?"

Each punch carries a timestamp and coordinates, compared against your registered office sites, and is tagged Office, Remote or field automatically. The goal is not monitoring people — it is that Office and Remote hours separate themselves, so nobody has to self-report at month-end.

"How do we get it onto everyone's device?"

You do not. ClocDot is a PWA — employees open a link and add it to the home screen. No app store, no MDM, no IT project. It works on phones, tablets and desktops, keeps working offline, and syncs the queued punches when the connection comes back.

FAQ

Remote attendance FAQ

How is remote clock-in different from a traditional time clock?

A traditional time clock is tied to a physical device or the company network, so employees have to be on site. Remote clock-in runs on the employee's own phone or laptop, so it works from home, from a client site or from another country — and each punch is tagged with where it came from.

Do employees working from home still need attendance records?

Yes. Obligations do not disappear because the work happens off-site — records still need to be logged daily and to the minute, and retained for a number of years. Reporting in a chat group is generally not enough, because those messages are scattered through a conversation rather than kept as a reviewable record. See the compliance section. General information only, not legal advice.

Can employees see their own attendance records?

Yes — employees see their complete punch history and hours after logging in, without filing a request with HR. Records live in the cloud, so they survive a replaced laptop, a broken time clock or a lost paper card, and HR can export the full history as CSV whenever it is needed.

Can we switch from a physical time clock to remote clock-in?

Yes, and nothing needs replacing, because remote clock-in runs on devices your staff already own. Setup means registering office locations and working hours, then asking employees to add the PWA to their home screen. Most teams start the same day, and you can run one team in parallel before switching everyone.

Can office days and remote days use different clock-in methods?

Yes, and it is the most common hybrid setup: on office days require a connection to the company network, so the punch confirms someone is on site; on remote days use GPS to record the location and tag the punch as Remote. Both sources are stored per punch, and Office and Remote hours stay separate in the export.
